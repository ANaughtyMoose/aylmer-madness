# Aylmer Madness: story direction and an easy editing process
21 September 2026. Editorial proposal only. No mission/gameplay edits made.

## My recommendation

Keep the existing premise: Tom has a Ranger, an approaching September and a reason to earn money. Let the missions show what having that truck means to the people around him. Sometimes it is useful, sometimes it makes him the default ride, and sometimes it enables an idea nobody should have agreed to. The accumulating story is the history between these people, revealed through errands and repeated places.

We do not need a new plot to justify the game. We need better reasons for the existing trips and more recognisable people inside the cars. The emotional change can be small: at first the truck lets Tom go places; by the end, each place has something attached to it. This is a proposed reading, not a claim about Thomas's life.

## What already works

The strongest briefs are concrete and economical: Mike wants the couch in the tree; Sayyad isn't answering; Russell needs another ride from the golf club; Margaret has dental work to finish. They create a question the player wants answered without a cutscene. The canot purchase and its $45 price are another strong, tangible starting point.

The existing $1,200 goal, fuel costs and repairs give ordinary errands enough stakes. Keep the truck in Tom's ownership. Don't escalate into a villain, a disaster, a choice between friends and ambition, or losing everything at the finish.

The strongest specific life missions appear more promising than generic delivery chains. They should guide the quality of the writing across the game, without promoting or reordering them.

## Three problems to solve first

1. Conflicting sources. Runtime has 28 campaign jobs plus five interleaved arc beats; the older 18-job JSON is a different outline. Old borrowed-truck copy contradicts current ownership. Margaret's dental-work role sits beside generic history/bingo depictions. Reconcile copy after asking Thomas; don't replace gameplay.
2. Similar voices. Too many people call everything a terrible car, mock the driver and speak in the same heightened slang. Give each person a distinct rhythm, concern and form of kindness, based on actual anecdotes. Real people can be funny without delivering a punchline every time.
3. Imposed meaning. Some older dialogue states an entire philosophy of leaving Aylmer. A practical action or brief exchange will often feel more true. Don't turn every trip into a speech about youth or departure.

## How you guide this without doing homework

Send a voice note or paragraph in any order. Start with “the Russell story,” “this is what Sayyad was like,” or “that shop is wrong.” No template required. I'll return:
1. What I understood, preserving exact quotes separately.
2. The existing mission it fits.
3. A brief before/after for setup, one exchange and completion.
4. One unresolved question only if it changes the edit.

Your response can simply be KEEP, TWEAK, WRONG, or MORE STORY. A rejected detail goes on a “do not reintroduce” list. We should keep approved story separate from proposed copy and implemented changes.

## Highest-value input to make the game better

1. Tell three anchor stories in detail: the couch, Russell at the Royal Ottawa, and an ordinary Ranger errand. Real words and small mishaps are more valuable than a polished plot.
2. Describe how each central person actually spoke: language, pace, recurring phrase, what they cared about, and how they showed affection. Especially clarify Margaret's relationship to Tom and the distinct voices of Sayyad/Zahra.
3. Give a “wrong nostalgia” list: businesses you never visited, music you didn't hear, things you wouldn't say, invented character traits. Removing false familiarity is as valuable as adding detail.
4. Approve one 10-minute slice before expanding writing: the alternator trip plus the next existing beat. Listen for text interrupting directions, radio or a good quiet moment. No new mission needed.
5. For place atmosphere, share one normal day: meeting spot, actual order, route, radio choice, driveway sounds, what you did when nothing was planned. These give environment and audio teams useful specifics.
6. Judge with three questions after a drive: “Did I recognise someone?”, “Did I know why I was going there?”, and “Did anything feel made up?” Make edits from those answers.
